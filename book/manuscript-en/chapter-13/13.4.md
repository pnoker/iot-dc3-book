## 13.4 Decentralized IoT Architecture


### 13.4.1 The Architectural Shift from Centralized to Decentralized

The trust dilemma of the centralized IoT architecture was discussed earlier. The problem lies not merely in single points of failure or data silos, but in the fact that this is at its core a "brokered trust" model: every device depends on the centralized platform for identity authentication, message routing, and rule arbitration. As the IoT grows in scale, the maintenance cost, security risk, and system bottlenecks of this model amplify in step. The decentralized architecture is proposed to replace brokered trust with technical consensus.

**The architectural difference, seen through the trust model.** The fundamental divide between the two architectures is where the trust anchor sits. The centralized architecture concentrates all trust in one certification authority or cloud platform — if the platform is trustworthy, the system is secure; if the platform is compromised or its operator introduces malicious logic, the trust system collapses. The decentralized architecture instead disperses trust across multiple nodes in the network, using a distributed ledger and a consensus mechanism to bring all participants to agreement on state without depending on any single entity. One device transacting with another no longer needs the cloud platform to verify identity first — the distributed ledger itself provides the infrastructure for identity verification and transaction trust, so devices can interact directly at the peer-to-peer level.

**The engineering trade-off in scalability.** The scalability of a centralized architecture is linear and predictable: add servers, add bandwidth, add database shards — a mature pattern proven at internet scale. The scalability of a decentralized architecture faces a fundamental contradiction: consensus requires every full node to independently verify and store the entire ledger, so the more nodes there are, the greater the network communication complexity and storage cost. This is precisely the core engineering difficulty blockchain runs into in large-scale IoT deployments. Solutions have split into two paths: one reduces the load on the main chain through sharding, sidechains, or Layer 2 protocols; the other abandons the linear chain structure in favor of a directed acyclic graph (DAG). IOTA's "Tangle" is an example of the latter: every new transaction approves two historical transactions, transactions need no miners to package them, there is no concept of blocks, and no transaction fees exist — which makes sensor-level micro-transactions economically feasible.

**Gateway autonomy merged with edge computing.** Along the transition path, full decentralization does not arrive in one step. The more pragmatic approach introduces limited autonomy at the edge gateway layer, as an intermediate state between centralization and distribution. The gateway maintains a local device list and rule engine, and anchors only the key digital assets (device registration, firmware hashes, digests of major events) on-chain for global consistency. High-frequency real-time data is processed locally at the gateway, while the chain holds nothing but tamper-proof evidence fingerprints. In engineering terms, this "edge computing + blockchain" hybrid is more mature than having every sensor run a light node directly, and it integrates with existing centralized platforms far more easily and incrementally.

**The choice of consensus mechanism depends on the system boundary.** There is no universally optimal consensus. Proof of Work (PoW) withstands DoS attacks, but its computational intensity and energy consumption are unacceptable for IoT endpoints — although the requester's work is theoretically feasible, the computing resources it demands far exceed what large populations of embedded devices can offer. Practical solutions better suited to the IoT usually unfold in permissioned environments: a pre-defined set of trusted validator nodes achieves second-level block production and low-overhead verification. An ordering service further reduces consensus to a Byzantine fault tolerance variant — PBFT (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance) — among organization-level nodes. Choosing a consensus is not a matter of technical sophistication, but of the system's tolerance for latency, throughput, node count, and trust assumptions.

**Three stages of architectural evolution.** In practice, full decentralization is not pursued in a single leap. The more common evolutionary path runs: the first stage keeps the centralized platform and introduces on-chain fingerprint verification of core business data (batch information, key operation logs), serving as a trusted audit layer; the second stage migrates device registration and identity management onto the chain, replacing platform self-issued device tokens with decentralized identifiers (DID) while edge gateways keep the local device list consistent with the on-chain ledger; the third stage settles device-to-device transactions directly on a chain that relies on no intermediary — a step that requires solving the consensus mechanism and throughput bottlenecks. Most IoT projects stop at the first or second stage; only high-frequency, atomized scenarios such as micro-payments and energy trading need to advance to the third.

Engineering judgment: the value of a decentralized architecture lies not in technical sophistication, but in whether it lowers the trust cost of multi-party collaboration. If the participants covered by the system sit inside the same trust domain (for example, devices internal to a single enterprise), the simplicity of the centralized architecture is actually the advantage. Only when participants are independent of one another, cannot establish trust in advance, and their transaction history must be transparent to auditors do the engineering benefits of a decentralized architecture truly emerge. Once the boundary is judged clearly, architecture selection will not degenerate into technology worship.

### 13.4.2 Lightweight Consensus and Device Resource Adaptation

> This section is an engineering comparison of consensus mechanisms, for reference during selection. If you do not need to go deep into the internals of consensus protocols, you can jump directly to Section 13.5 (the AI + blockchain + IoT triangle paradigm) without affecting your understanding of the book's main storyline.

The consensus mechanism is the skeleton of a decentralized architecture, but the PoW of mainstream public chains is nearly unusable in IoT environments. A Raspberry Pi has less than one-millionth the computing power of a dedicated mining rig, and a battery-powered sensor node simply cannot sustain continuous hashing. Once PoW is abandoned, the question engineering teams face becomes more concrete: under the triple constraint of computing, storage, and energy, what kind of consensus can maintain distributed trust without burning through the devices' resources?

**PoA and the Trusted-Node Model**

The essence of Proof of Authority (PoA) is replacing computing power with identity. Pre-selected validator nodes lock up reputation or collateral and take turns producing blocks, with no hash competition. Validator identities are public and their behavior is supervised by the network — a misbehaving node is evicted according to the rules and loses its collateral. The decentralized architecture was originally characterized by peer-to-peer communication and no third-party verification; PoA introduces trust delegation under this premise.

The direct benefit of PoA in the IoT is that TPS is no longer bound by computing power; it depends only on inter-node network latency and signing performance. An ARM-based edge gateway running a PoA consensus node can handle tens to hundreds of TPS — enough for medium-scale device interactions. The price is a compromise toward "limited decentralization": when the validator set is too concentrated or controlled by a single entity, the network faces collusion risk. In consortium chains or multi-party-governed industrial networks, this price is usually acceptable.

**The DAG Structure: IOTA's Tangle**

The Tangle proposed by IOTA is a distributed ledger structure designed for the IoT. It does not string "chained blocks" together; instead, it requires every new transaction to actively verify two previous transactions. Transactions are linked through a directed acyclic graph: the more active the network, the more verification branches, and the higher the security. This bypasses the bottleneck that makes traditional blockchains hard to run on low-power devices.

One engineering merit of the Tangle is "zero transaction fees." On the Bitcoin chain, the fee for a small transfer can exceed the value of the data itself, which is unworkable in IoT micro-transaction scenarios. IOTA has devices verify other transactions themselves; there are no miners, and therefore no concept of fees. As one source puts it: "Sensors can offer reporting services to many MQTT subscribers. The service is valuable in aggregate, but the value per transaction is so small that the Bitcoin fee for delivering the information could exceed the value of the data itself." The Tangle was proposed precisely to resolve this contradiction.

The Tangle's engineering limits are equally evident. When network activity is insufficient, a new transaction lacks enough verified transactions to confirm its validity, which leaves the Tangle facing a security compromise during its bootstrapping phase or in low-density networks. Devices must be online to participate in verification; offline, they cannot produce a valid Tangle transaction.

**PBFT Variants and Adaptation to Constrained Resources**

PBFT is the classic state machine replication algorithm, often adopted by consortium chains. Standard PBFT requires all nodes to exchange three-phase voting messages, with a communication complexity of O(n²). Once the number of nodes exceeds a few dozen, or inter-node links carry significant latency, performance degrades rapidly.

Improvements aimed at the IoT concentrate on two directions: one is dynamic sharding that partitions the device population into small consensus groups to contain communication complexity; the other introduces accelerator nodes based on trusted execution environments, moving part of the voting logic into hardware security modules to shorten confirmation latency. In practice, device roles call for distinguishing validator nodes from light nodes — a light node only submits transactions and receives confirmations without voting in consensus, which lets a federated architecture accommodate large numbers of resource-constrained edge sensors. The core direction is the same: assign the trust-verification task to the few nodes with sufficient resources, and leave the many weak endpoints with nothing but data submission.

**Bringing in Hardware Security Modules**

Whether in PoA's signing step or in key generation for Tangle nodes, protecting the private key is the precondition of consensus security. IoT devices generally lack hardware security enclaves; their private keys sit exposed in flash or RAM over the long term, and once extracted by an attacker, the node's identity can be hijacked. The more pragmatic approach is to integrate a dedicated security chip at the gateway level (such as a TPM or a secure element) and move signing operations inside the chip. Once written, the private key can never be read out; transactions can only be signed through API calls. The chip's added cost is controllable in volume production, but deploying it only on the validator side is a reasonable engineering trade-off — equipping every single sensor would cost far too much.

**Summary of Engineering Judgment**

Consensus selection does not follow the single dimension of "the more decentralized, the better." The real constraints of IoT networks require engineering teams to weigh device computing power, deployment density, transaction frequency, and operational governance relationships at the same time. PoA suits industrial consortium chains — its trust model leans centralized, but the engineering change required to deploy it is the smallest. The Tangle can support high-density micro-transactions but cannot get off to a safe start in sparse networks. PBFT variants better fit closed networks with strict final-consistency requirements and a controllable node count. No universal consensus exists that suits every IoT scenario — the first step of engineering practice is to identify the trust model of the deployment environment, and only then match the consensus mechanism to it.

### 13.4.3 Interconnecting Heterogeneous Blockchain Networks

> This section discusses cross-chain technology (atomic swaps, relay chains, oracles), which is advanced blockchain engineering material. The book's main storyline centers on Section 13.2 (device identity and DID) and Section 13.3 (supply-chain traceability); read the cross-chain details as needed.

In IoT scenarios, blockchain deployment will never be a single network. In a typical smart city architecture, device identity may run on a consortium chain, supply-chain data may be recorded on another permissioned chain, and some publicly certified data may be anchored to a public chain for broader verification. When these networks stay isolated from one another, the IoT data loop is cut apart: an identity a device registered on chain A cannot be recognized by contracts on chain B, and a cross-organization production record must be verified repeatedly across multiple systems. In engineering terms, meeting this need for heterogeneous network interconnection centers on letting different chains understand one another and transfer data and assets in a trustworthy manner.

**Atomic swaps and hash locking.**  
This is the most direct technical path for cross-chain transactions. The core idea: a user on chain A locks an asset or data credential and generates a hash; a user on chain B creates a lock contract for the corresponding hash, redeemable only by presenting the preimage. If both sides unlock within the agreed time, the transaction succeeds; if either side defaults, the asset automatically returns to its original chain.

An atomic swap relies on no third-party relay — only on hash functions and timeout logic. The cost is its dependence on the smart-contract capabilities of both chains, and its inability to cross arbitrary data formats or complex states. **Relay chains and cross-chain protocols.**  
When higher throughput or more general message passing is required, the relay chain becomes the more mature engineering choice. The relay chain is itself an independent blockchain that maintains light nodes or state summaries of the participating chains. Chain A submits a cross-chain transaction to the relay chain; validators on the relay chain verify the transaction's authenticity by running a light client of chain A, then generate a proof on the relay chain. Chain B fetches that proof from the relay chain and executes the corresponding operation on its own chain. Cosmos's IBC protocol is the exemplar of this architecture: each participating chain, at startup, sets up a light-client channel connected to the relay chain, and a two-round "submit-confirm" handshake guarantees finality of cross-chain messages.

For the IoT, the relay chain's key advantage is that it decouples the cross-chain protocol from device resource limits. An IoT node on a participating chain needs to run only finality verification, not process the full data of other chains in real time.

**Oracle integration.**  
In a good many IoT scenarios, heterogeneous network interconnection involves no asset transfer at all — what is needed is verifiable external data from one blockchain to trigger a contract on another. For example, an irrigation contract deployed on a public chain depends on soil-moisture sensor data stored on a permissioned chain. A relay chain cannot be used directly here, because the data on the permissioned chain is not a chain-native asset. The oracle then acts as the data bridge: it reads the data from the permissioned chain, generates a cryptographic proof containing the data content (using that chain's endorsement signatures and block hash), and submits the proof together with the original data to the receiving contract on the public chain. The public-chain contract decides whether to accept the data by verifying that the signatures and hashes are consistent with the latest permissioned-chain block header.

When implementing this kind of "data-read cross-chain," the trustworthiness of the oracle nodes themselves becomes the source of risk. Solutions usually aggregate results from multiple independent oracle nodes (via threshold signatures or majority voting) to reduce the risk of any single oracle being subverted.

**Case study: a cross-chain bridge for IoT device identity migration.**  
Consider an automotive parts supplier whose core production-process data is recorded on an internal permissioned chain network. Once a part leaves the factory and is delivered to the vehicle manufacturer, the manufacturer wants to verify the part's production batch and quality-inspection status on its own public chain. The cross-chain bridge scheme works roughly as follows:

1. On the permissioned chain, completing quality inspection triggers a transaction containing the part's serial number, batch number, and hash, endorsed by multiple authorized nodes.
2. The cross-chain bridge's relay nodes continuously listen to a dedicated channel on the permissioned chain, extract new transactions, and generate SPV proofs.
3. The relay node submits the proof and transaction data to a bridge contract deployed on the public chain.
4. The bridge contract verifies the permissioned chain's set of endorsement signatures and the block hash; once verification passes, it mints a trusted token on the public chain representing the part's identity.

From then on, any contract on the public chain can directly verify the part's origin. This single case shows the core engineering value of heterogeneous network interconnection: it connects blockchains across different trust domains, keeping IoT data trustworthy as it flows across organizations through the full lifecycle.

**Engineering judgment.** Choosing a cross-chain scheme requires evaluating the degree of heterogeneity and the trust model. Atomic swaps suit lightweight migration of assets or credentials and introduce no additional trust, but they do not carry complex state. The relay-chain architecture suits scenarios that need general-purpose cross-chain messaging, but participating chains must maintain light clients, placing high demands on each chain's protocol and on-chain governance. Oracle integration is the primary choice for data transfer between consortium chains and public chains, and here the design focus should fall on how decentralized the oracle nodes are and on the data verification workflow. Whatever the scheme, connecting heterogeneous networks must not break each chain's consensus security boundary — the goal of interconnection is to extend the scope of trust, not to replace one chain's trust model with another's.
