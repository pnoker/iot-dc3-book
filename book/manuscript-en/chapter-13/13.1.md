## 13.1 Overview of Blockchain and IoT Convergence

### 13.1.1 The Trust Dilemma of Centralized IoT Architecture

On the shop floor of a smart factory, dozens of temperature and humidity sensors, vibration monitors, and robotic arm controllers aggregate their data and receive their commands through a single cloud platform. One day, the platform's backend database is maliciously altered: the cooling system's trigger threshold is tampered from 80 °C down to 50 °C, and an entire production line halts without warning. The incident investigation consumes three shifts, and the more intractable problem is this — no one can prove, at the moment it matters, that the data was tampered with. The platform operator says "this is the raw data the devices reported"; the device vendor insists "the sensor readings were normal." Each side holds to its own account, and there is no neutral arbiter.

This is not alarmism. The vast majority of IoT systems today depend on a centralized broker communication model, also known as the client-server model. Under this model, every device's data reporting, authentication, and command dispatch must pass through the cloud server. Even two devices a few centimeters apart must route their communication up to the cloud and back. This architecture was a reasonable choice in the early days of computers and the Internet, but as the number of connected devices climbs rapidly from the billions toward the hundreds of billions, the problems amplify sharply.

**Single point of failure and security risk** is the first problem put on the table. The centralized server cluster is the system's sole hub; once that hub is attacked, goes down, or suffers network fluctuation, the entire IoT network can be paralyzed. More fatally, this hub is also the attacker's ideal breach point — compromising one central node amounts to obtaining administrative control over every device. Decentralized approaches can eliminate precisely this class of single-point failure at the root, but under centralized architecture the balance of forces between attack and defense is asymmetric: the defense must hold dozens of entry points, while the attacker needs to find only one crack. In addition, a centralized platform's scheduled maintenance windows, version upgrades, and certificate rotations all cause service interruptions; in a model that depends on a single trust anchor, such interruptions are nearly unavoidable — during a maintenance window every device sits in a "black box" state, with prior notification and post-hoc recovery resting entirely on the platform team's one-sided promises.

**Data silos and tampering risk** follow close behind. In a centralized IoT platform, the raw data generated by devices first flows into the platform's own database and is then exposed to upper-layer applications through APIs. This process inherently builds data barriers: device data from different vendors and different platforms is hard to share or cross-validate. More seriously, once data enters the central database, it escapes the device owner's control. A database administrator, or any third party with server access, is fully capable of modifying, deleting, or forging records; in a subsequent audit, because there is no tamper-proof ledger, altered data is difficult to distinguish from the original. Even if the platform provides complete operation logs, those logs themselves sit on the same server — what auditors see is still the "platform-approved" version, not the original truth in any technical sense. It is as if you handed your account book to someone for safekeeping and asked him to keep honest books, while the book and its seal both remained in that same person's hands.

**High cross-party trust costs** are the deeper drag on large-scale IoT deployment. The participants along a single supply chain may include raw-material suppliers, manufacturers, logistics providers, distributors, retailers, and end users, each running its own information system. To get these systems to agree on the same set of data, the traditional approach is to bring in an authoritative third-party platform or regulator to centrally verify and distribute the data. The result of that approach is that every participant pays steep integration, audit, and legal costs, and the response speed of the whole process degrades noticeably. When something goes wrong in one link — a temperature anomaly in one batch's reefer truck, say — the parties spend enormous time establishing "whose data is trustworthy" rather than "whether the data itself is true." Trust is passed along through layer upon layer of contracts and after-the-fact accountability, with no technical foundation on which every participant can verify independently and in real time.

These three problems are, in essence, the absence of a **trust mechanism**. Under centralized architecture, the sole anchor of trust is the platform operator, and the operator's "trustworthiness" rests on institutions, contracts, and after-the-fact accountability — not on any guarantee native to the technology itself. Consider a concrete scenario: a smart-home platform suffers an insider attack; through the back end, the attacker modifies the authorization whitelist of a smart door lock, and the doors of hundreds of households open by themselves late at night. Afterwards, the platform produces a server log, but users have every reason to question whether that log, too, was tampered with — because the log itself is stored on the same server. In a structure where one party is "both athlete and referee," device identity, data authenticity, and command provenance can all be swapped in the dark, and technology alone can hardly prove innocence. Moving trust from a single centralized node onto a distributed ledger — jointly maintained by multiple parties, protected by cryptography, and immutable — is the key starting point for breaking this triple dilemma. This is precisely the foothold from which blockchain enters the IoT.

### 13.1.2 How Blockchain Rebuilds the IoT Trust Mechanism

Return to the smart-factory failure that opened this section — if the record of the cooling system's threshold change had been public, immutable, and jointly witnessed by every node on the network, assigning responsibility would no longer be an endless dispute. The platform operator could not deny "the threshold was changed," and the device vendor could not claim "the sensor data was fabricated." This is the most direct value blockchain brings to the IoT: it shifts trust away from dependence on a single institution onto the underlying technology and network consensus.

Blockchain is in essence a distributed ledger technology (DLT). It does not live on any central server; instead, multiple nodes on the network each hold a complete copy. Whenever new data is produced, participants confirm its validity through a consensus mechanism, pack it into a new "block," and cryptographically link that block to its predecessor. This continuous chain of transaction records is nearly impossible to tamper with — the core engineering property of blockchain. Mapped onto the IoT, blockchain rebuilds the foundation of trust along three dimensions.

**Dimension one: the distributed ledger delivers global data consistency**

The most common dispute over a centralized platform is "has the data been touched." In a blockchain network, every participant — device manufacturer, platform operator, data-analysis provider, end customer — can run a node and hold a fully synchronized copy of the ledger. When a device reports a temperature reading, that record is broadcast to the whole network. Under the joint witness of all nodes, once it is confirmed and written into a block, it carries the endorsement of the entire network. Any node that tries a local modification will be caught by the other nodes as a ledger inconsistency and rejected; the tampering attempt itself becomes a new anomalous record. From an engineering standpoint, this solves the most persistent headache of centralized databases: once data is on chain, it gains global consistency and historical non-repudiation.

**Dimension two: digital signatures and consensus make device identity trustworthy**

In the IoT, the question "who is this device" has long depended on a centralized certificate authority (CA) or on keys distributed by the platform. In blockchain-based schemes, each device holds a pair of asymmetric keys — a public key and a private key. The public key serves as the device's on-chain address, visible to the whole network; the private key resides in secure hardware such as a TPM secure chip or an SE secure element. When the device sends data, it generates a digital signature with its private key; any node can verify the signature against the public key. A passed verification establishes that the message truly comes from the device it claims to be, and that its content has not been altered.

This identity verification is carried out in a decentralized way — through the consensus mechanism (IoT scenarios commonly use Proof of Authority, PBFT variants, or DAG structures, rather than the hugely resource-hungry Proof of Work), network nodes jointly witness every authentication and data submission instead of depending on a single CA box. Given the vast number of participants in an IoT system, some of whom may be malicious, such a multi-witness mechanism is necessary. In 2017, for example, academic research validated the feasibility of blockchain-based device identity management; its core idea was precisely to use the public key as the unique identifier and bind it to an on-chain device description document.

**Dimension three: smart contracts execute trust rules automatically**

A great deal of IoT business logic takes the form "if a condition is met, execute an action automatically" — for example, "if the temperature exceeds the threshold and stays there for a while, start the cooling system," or "if a logistics truck enters the warehouse perimeter, open the loading dock." Under centralized architecture these rules are executed by backend business-logic servers; once such a server is attacked or misconfigured, the rules can be bypassed or tampered with.

A smart contract deploys this kind of rule on the blockchain as code. The contract code is public and immutable. Data reported by devices serves as the trigger condition. When the on-chain data satisfies the contract's preset logic, the contract automatically executes the agreed operation; the computation is verified jointly by all nodes on the network, and the result is non-repudiable. This "code as trust" paradigm lets device-coordination activities be defined in advance and audited afterwards. For example, a temperature sensor writes its reading on chain; the cooling-system contract reads the value, judges whether it exceeds the threshold, and, if it does, automatically sends an open command to the valve controller — the whole process requires no human intervention, and every step leaves an immutable record.

These three dimensions — data consistency, trustworthy identity, and automated execution — together constitute blockchain's reconstruction of the IoT trust mechanism. To be clear, the trust anchor blockchain provides is the immutability and traceable provenance of on-chain data; whether the data a sensor perceived was true at the moment it was produced (a physically tampered temperature-probe reading, for instance) is not something blockchain can directly guarantee. Trustworthiness of source data before it goes on chain must be resolved in concert with edge computing, gateway verification, and even artificial intelligence.

Blockchain is no silver bullet. Its introduction brings new engineering challenges: storage and compute resource consumption far above centralized schemes, constrained transaction throughput (especially on PoW chains), loss of ownership when a private key is lost, and more. The sections that follow in this chapter discuss each of these in turn and give mitigation strategies. But viewed as a trust-building mechanism, blockchain — by rebuilding the rules at the data layer, the identity layer, and the business-execution layer — lays the trust foundation for the IoT's step from "everything interconnected" to "everything intelligently interconnected."

### 13.1.3 Evolution Trends and Engineering Challenges of the Converged Architecture

Trust is not free. The gap between a sensor reporting one reading and a blockchain confirming one transaction — in frequency, in payload size, in latency tolerance — decides that "putting all data on chain" is an engineering non-starter. The converged architecture has therefore evolved through three stages of compromise, each one a trade-off between resource cost and strength of trust.

**Stage one: off-chain storage + on-chain hash.** Raw data (high-frequency temperature series, video streams, large files) stays in local storage or a centralized data lake; only a digest of the data (a SHA-256, say) goes on chain. Verification recomputes the hash and compares. This scheme cuts on-chain storage cost dramatically, but it can only prove the data "has not been tampered with," not that it "was true at the instant of capture" — the latter requires working with edge verification (see Chapter 8). The root of trust still rests partly on the data source.

**Stage two: light nodes, partially on chain.** As light nodes, IoT devices synchronize only block headers, hold no full ledger, and submit transactions with the help of full nodes. For storage-constrained embedded devices (ARM Cortex-M series MCUs, for instance), this is a workable compromise. A dedicated chain using proof of authority (PoA) can produce blocks within seconds and keeps compute requirements low. But transactions that occur while a device is offline must rely on a local cache, and after-the-fact synchronization introduces temporal inconsistency. The light-node approach essentially trades "trusting full nodes" for savings in storage and bandwidth — a partial retreat from decentralization, yet still the most practical access path today for resource-constrained devices.

**Stage three: fully on-chain coordination.** Devices take part in consensus and execute smart contracts directly, achieving genuinely decentralized autonomous collaboration. At present this scheme exists mainly in experiments and high-value automation scenarios. A public blockchain's transaction throughput is limited by its consensus design, and a single block's data capacity and block interval cannot carry high-frequency writes from massive fleets of IoT devices — the performance bottleneck is plain.

**Key engineering challenges and response paths**

- **Sharding**: split the network into multiple parallel sub-chains, which in theory scales throughput linearly, but the atomicity and latency problems of cross-shard communication are not yet fully engineered. Sharding requires a node to hold the complete state of one shard, so the storage demands on devices remain high.
- **Sidechains and Layer 2 scaling**: high-frequency transactions complete in off-chain channels, and only the final state hash is committed to the main chain. State channels, for example, let devices exchange small amounts of resources off chain, but funds must be locked in advance and settlement risk borne. State channels and Plasma are a better fit for IoT scenarios with low-frequency settlement.
- **Dedicated IoT blockchain platforms**: some permissioned chains support dynamic node joining through pluggable consensus. Other schemes adopt DAG structures, with design goals including near-zero transaction fees and asynchronous writes. IOTA's Tangle, for one, requires each new transaction to validate the two transactions before it; early on it depended on a centralized Coordinator node, and the subsequent Coordicide project aims to remove that dependency. **Hidden costs and design constraints**

Storage bloat: every participant keeps a complete ledger, and a size of tens of GB is entirely infeasible on an ARM Cortex-M. The common industry practice is for the device to keep only an index of its own transactions and entrust the full ledger to a cloud node — in essence, a partial retreat from decentralization.

Privacy exposure: blockchain's transparency conflicts with the privacy of enterprise business data. Some permissioned-chain platforms isolate private ledgers through a channel mechanism; a few public-chain schemes offer shielded channels.

The converged architecture will not move toward "fully on chain" in the short term; it will stratify by asset value, data sensitivity, and latency tolerance. The sections that follow work out concrete schemes for device identity, data trustworthiness, and supply-chain traceability.
