## 3.8 Sensing-Layer Engineering Summary

### 3.8.1 Engineering Wrap-up and the Practice Checklist

Technology choices in the sensing layer directly determine the engineering boundaries of an IoT project. If sensor accuracy is insufficient, however well the upper-layer algorithms run, no valid data gets in; if RFID anti-collision is handled poorly, a warehouse auto-inventory system will print wrong lists in tag-dense areas; if a positioning solution develops blind zones at indoor-outdoor handovers, a mobile robot's path planning will suffer intermittent dropouts. These problems are rarely emphasized in product manuals, yet they are unavoidable pitfalls in field deployment.

Along the main line of "physical sensing — identity recognition — spatial positioning — edge processing — local intelligence — unified abstraction," this chapter has taken apart the sensing layer's core technologies. Every area carries a clear engineering trade-off: sensor selection balances accuracy, power consumption, and interface; the RFID band directly determines read/write range and scenario fit; a positioning solution must first understand the innate blind zones of single-source technologies, then fill them in with fusion; an edge node's compute and power budget together decide the model complexity it can carry; the heart of on-device AI is not "which algorithm to move onto the MCU" but whether the accuracy loss after quantization is acceptable; and the crux of thing-model design lies in balancing semantic consistency against extensibility.

The most effective way to turn what you have learned from knowledge into action is to build an engineering checklist. Pull it out and verify item by item every time you plan a sensing layer, and the odds of stumbling in the field drop sharply. Table 3-8-1 covers the full process from selection and deployment through model design.

| Area | Check item | Self-check guidance |
|------|--------|----------|
| Sensor selection | Physical-quantity range and headroom | Against the range being measured, reserve at least 1.5× headroom; watch for nonlinear offset, and prefer models that were linearization-calibrated at the factory (e.g., MEMS pressure sensors with TC compensation). |
| Sensor interface | Analog/digital choice and wiring | Analog signals (e.g., 4-20 mA, 0-10 V) must match the ADC's effective bit width, with the shield grounded at a single end; for digital interfaces (e.g., I²C, SPI), mind address conflicts and bus capacitive-load limits. |
| Signal conditioning | Sampling rate and effective noise bit width | For fast-changing signals (e.g., vibration, current transients), sample at least 2.5× the bandwidth; focus on the effective number of bits (ENOB), not the nominal resolution. |
| RFID band selection | Environmental compatibility and tag cost | Metal and liquid surfaces do not suit the UHF band (860-960 MHz) — switch to HF (13.56 MHz) or low frequency (125 kHz); for active tags, evaluate the battery-replacement cycle and cost. |
| Anti-collision protocol | Stability under bulk read/write | Once tag volume exceeds 200 tags/s, evaluate the Q algorithm (EPC Gen2) or improved framed-slotted ALOHA; prefer readers that support dynamic frame-length adjustment. |
| Positioning fusion | Single-source blind zones and fallback plans | When GNSS loses lock indoors, switch to UWB/Wi-Fi fingerprinting; when initializing the Kalman filter, mind the warm start of the covariance matrix to avoid first-fix drift. |
| Edge-node hardware | Compute match and power budget | Look past CPU clock frequency — check for an integrated NPU or DSP (e.g., STM32N6, i.MX8M Plus); confirm peak power stays within the supply (e.g., PoE++ at 60 W, or battery capacity). |
| Data preprocessing | Filter real-time behavior | Moving averages introduce phase delay — prefer Kalman filtering or a first-order lag filter in real-time control scenarios; when the sampling rate exceeds twice the signal bandwidth, apply a hardware anti-aliasing filter first. |
| On-device AI deployment | Model quantization and the calibration set | Converting from float32 to int8 requires a calibration set (roughly 100-500 samples) to compute the scaling factors, keeping post-quantization accuracy loss under 2%; prefer hardware that supports TFLite Micro or TensorRT. |
| Adaptive sampling | Threshold setting and historical data | A threshold too small raises sampling power consumption, one too large lets events slip through; aggregate 2-4 weeks of historical data and use mean ± 2σ as the dynamic threshold baseline; for long-cycle variation scenarios, introduce an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA). |
| Thing-model design | Service and event coverage | Check whether Property, Service, and Event elements are complete; IoT DC3 maps device services to dispatchable commands (Command), and a missing write service or alarm event needed for closed-loop control leaves the upper layers unable to drive the device. |
| Data-interoperability verification | Device shadow sync | Use the IoT DC3 thing-model management interface (see the official API documentation) to test the bidirectional synchronization of the device shadow, ensuring property changes reach the cloud within 5 seconds. |

For the learning path ahead, take a data-acquisition project you already have as a training ground and try applying on-device AI and thing-model thinking to it directly. When you revisit RFID identity mapping or the details of indoor fusion positioning, go back to the theory discussions in Sections 3.4 and 3.5; when making edge-node deployment and preprocessing trade-offs, Section 3.6 has a more detailed discussion. The sensing layer's data ultimately flows to the application layer, and later chapters will progressively take up data cleaning, modeling, and closed-loop actuation.

Finally, return to the starting point of sensing-layer design. The questions below have no standard answers, but they can help you pin down where your decisions start and what constrains them:

- Which sensing principle fits this physical quantity best, and are accuracy and cost acceptable at the same time?
- For identification, is RFID, QR codes, or mechanical marking the better deal? What are the tag cost and the read/write reliability, respectively?
- Is position information an aid to decisions, or must it be reliable? Can you accept the latency that fused positioning introduces?
- Upload all the raw data, or refine it at the edge first and then decide what goes to the cloud? Does the edge node have enough compute to run the algorithms you want to run?
- Can the sensor decide on its own when to sample and when to sleep, or can it only run on a fixed cycle?
- Can same-type devices from different vendors be managed uniformly through one thing model? The IoT DC3 thing-model management interface has a complete API list in the references — verify item by item as you integrate.
